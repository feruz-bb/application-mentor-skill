# Recommendation Letters (English)

For Uzbek-language *tavsiyanoma* in a state-scholarship folder, use `uzbek-scholarship-documents.md` instead — it is a different document with different rules.

## Who should write it

In priority order: the **academic supervisor (ilmiy rahbar)**, a professor who has taught the student in a named course, a research or project supervisor, then a manager at an organisation where the student worked or volunteered. For undergraduate admission, subject teachers — ideally including one from the final year, from core subjects, in two different disciplines.

Two rules students get wrong:
- **A famous name who barely knows the student is worth less than an ordinary lecturer who does.** Avoid VIP and family-friend letters.
- **The student must not draft their own letter.** It is detectable, several systems require the recommender to submit directly, and Common App requires a signed FERPA waiver. When a professor asks the student to "write it and I'll sign," the right help is a *brief* — a page of dated facts, projects, grades and incidents the professor can draw on — not a finished letter.

## What the committee is actually looking for

Three things, in this order:

1. **Academic ability** — not the grades, which they already have, but the spark: whether the student is motivated by knowledge or by scores.
2. **Personal qualities and role in the community** — *the most important element*.
3. **A forecast** — who the recommender thinks this person will become.

Admissions read holistically: social position, access to resources, how competitive the environment was, and how hard the achievement was under those conditions. A recommender who explains that context is doing something the transcript cannot.

## Structure

1. **Opening: who is recommending, and the exact relationship.** Role + duration + named courses. This is mandatory — without it the letter has no weight.
   > *"I have known Kamila for over a year as her professor in Global Business Issues and Marketing Principles."*
   > *"I am glad to write this letter about the best student in my career, Rosa, to whom I taught Russian for 4 years from 8th to 11th grade."*
   > *"As a Program Assistant, I had the chance to work closely with Jasur throughout the program."*
2. **One or two dominant qualities, each carried by a story.** Not a list of virtues — one or two, with incidents.
3. **Evidence.** Behavioural, comparative, quantified.
4. **A ranking statement,** if it can be made honestly.
5. **Program-specific paragraph** — why this student fits *this* program.
6. **Close:** verdict → what the host gains → an offer to be contacted.
7. **Signature block:** handwritten signature, full name, title, institution, email, phone. Letterhead (rasmiy blanka) and stamp (muhr) where available.

Length: 350–500 words, one page.

## What strong evidence looks like

**Behavioural and comparative** — the best example in the corpus:
> *"She achieved the final grades of A0 for both classes… She never missed the class, unlike the other exchange students usually did… she faithfully reviewed the weekly class materials, and actively raised her questions and delivered constructive critique."*

**Third-party verified:**
> *"Selected from a very competitive pool of more than 1,300 started applications."*
> *"His supervisor rated him 'very satisfactory' across all key skills."*
> *"Co-authored an article published by the Uzbekistan National News Agency (UzA)."*

**Program-specific:**
> *"Her keen interest in courses such as Political Science and Political Systems of Post-Soviet Countries aligns well with the curriculum offered by the University of Economics in Bratislava."*

**Ranked, when deserved:**
> *"One of my three best Russian language students in twenty years."*
Only make this claim if it is true. Vague ranking — "above average" — actively harms; the reader hears the hedge.

## The three named mistakes

From the mentor's own teaching:

**1. Generalities instead of examples and numbers.**
❌ *"U yaxshi o'qiydi"*, *"Mas'uliyatli talaba"*
✅ *"Jasur loyihada 5 kishilik jamoaga yetakchilik qildi"*, *"u bir oy ichida tadqiqot natijasini 30% yaxshiladi"*

**2. Strengths asserted without proof.**
❌ *"U yaxshi muloqotga kirisha oladigan talaba."*
✅ *"U MUN konferensiyasida delegat sifatida ishtirok etib, 2 marta 'Eng kuchli delegat' nominatsiyasini qo'lga kiritgan."*

**3. The relationship left vague.**
❌ *"Men Jasurni yaxshi taniyman…"* — who is writing, for how long, in what capacity?
✅ *"Men Xalqaro huquq fakulteti o'qituvchisi sifatida ommaviy huquq fanidan ushbu talabaga dars berganman. Shuningdek, 2 yil davomida ilmiy rahbari bo'lganman."*

## Further failure modes

- **A résumé in prose.** Re-listing grades and awards already elsewhere in the application wastes the one document that could add something. *Fewer general facts, more key points.*
- **Praising "potential" with no achievement.** "He has great potential and an opportunity to develop" reads as: the teacher is not impressed.
- **Versatility instead of a zest.** *"Universities do not need balanced students, but balanced classes."* One vivid quality beats five listed ones.
- **Hackneyed vocabulary** — "hardworking", "smart", "responsible". Prefer: analytical, inquisitive, creative, mature, empathetic, principled.
- **A boring letter.** Admissions officers read these late at night in volume. A letter with no story in it is not read closely.
- **Pronoun errors.** One otherwise excellent letter in the corpus refers to a female student as "he" throughout a paragraph — a small error that undermines the claim of a close relationship.
- **Duplicating the student's own essay.** If the letter recycles the CV's competition list, it adds nothing.
- **"To Whom It May Concern"** when the target program is known — fine for a general reference, a missed opportunity otherwise.

## The MIT question set

A recommender stuck for material should answer these; so should a student preparing a brief for their professor:

- How did the relationship develop, and in what context?
- What unusual ability, talent or leadership have you observed?
- What motivates this student?
- How do they interact with teachers and with peers?
- How did they respond to disappointment or failure?
- Are there family or personal circumstances that give context to what they achieved?
- What will you remember most about them?
- Who do you see them becoming?

## Review checklist

- [ ] Recommender's role, relationship and duration stated in the first two sentences
- [ ] Named courses or projects, not just "I know the student"
- [ ] One or two qualities carried by concrete incidents
- [ ] At least one quantified or third-party-verified claim
- [ ] A ranking statement, if honest
- [ ] A paragraph connecting the student to *this* program
- [ ] Does not duplicate the CV or the student's own essay
- [ ] Correct pronouns and correct name throughout, including the program name
- [ ] Closing: verdict + benefit to host + contactability
- [ ] Full contact block: name, title, institution, email, phone
- [ ] Signature present; letterhead and stamp where the program expects them
- [ ] One page
- [ ] Submitted by the recommender, not forwarded by the student, where the system requires it
