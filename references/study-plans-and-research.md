# Study Plans, Research Proposals and Academic Articles

## Study plan

Asked for by exchange programs, government scholarships (Korea's NSP, Chinese and Japanese government scholarships, some Erasmus tracks) and most PhD applications. It is the most *structural* document a student will write — the reviewer is checking feasibility, not enthusiasm.

Observed shape from the strongest sample in the corpus:

1. **Header block** — Host University · Duration · Term
2. **Identification paragraph** — who you are, current institution, degree, in first person
3. **Research or study intent** — what you want to work on and why it needs this institution
4. **Course-by-course section in prose** — each course as a heading with its ECTS, and a paragraph on why it belongs in the plan
5. **Course table** — `Planned term | Course code | Course title | Description | Credit value`, totalling to the required credits (e.g. "Overall course value: 30")
6. **Extracurricular table** — `Activity | Date | Description`
7. No rhetorical conclusion; the plan ends when the tables end

Total ~1,000–1,500 words.

**What makes it credible:** real course codes and real credit values taken from the host's actual catalogue. This is the one document where a student cannot fake research — the codes either exist or they don't. If a student's draft has no course codes, that is the first assignment.

**What to check:**
- [ ] Credits total to the required number
- [ ] Course codes and titles match the host university's current catalogue
- [ ] Every course is justified against the stated goal, not just listed
- [ ] The plan is achievable in the stated duration
- [ ] Dates are internally consistent — a sample in the corpus described a 2024 event as "upcoming" inside a 2025 plan, which undermines everything around it
- [ ] Framing is about the work, not about the applicant's self-esteem or CV. *"Being a scholarship holder enhances my self-esteem"* is the wrong register for this document
- [ ] Extracurricular section connects to the academic plan rather than padding it

## Research proposal (PhD and research grants)

Structure: title · abstract · problem statement and its significance · literature review and the gap · research questions or hypotheses · methodology · expected outcomes and contribution · timeline · references · why this supervisor and this institution.

The two things reviewers actually test: whether the **gap** is real and specifically stated, and whether the **method** could produce an answer to the question asked. Ambition without method reads as naivety; method without a question reads as busywork.

Naming the intended supervisor and showing you have read their work is close to mandatory for PhD applications, and it is the single most common omission.

## Academic articles — IMRAD

Publications matter here beyond scholarship: Uzbek state scholarships require **at least two published articles or theses**, and for the Presidential stipend the topic must match the student's field. So article quality is often the binding constraint on eligibility.

**Structure `[D]`:** Title → authors and affiliations (ORCID where available) → Abstract → Keywords → **Introduction · Methods · Results · Discussion** → References. For theoretical work, "Methods" becomes "Theoretical Basis." **The order of these sections never changes.**

The four sections answer four questions: what problem? how was it studied? what was found? what does it mean?

- **Title** — 3–15 words, a label not a sentence; no abbreviations, formulas or coined terms.
- **Abstract** — one paragraph, 150–250 words: purpose, methods, results, main conclusions. Nothing may appear here that is not in the body. An abstract using 100 of 250 allowed words is incomplete.
- **Keywords** — 8–12, nominative case; they are how the paper is found.
- **Introduction** — four moves: establish context → review the literature → establish the gap → state the purpose. Define specialist terms and abbreviations here.
- **Methods** — detailed enough that a competent specialist could reproduce the work. Reviewers reject good papers whose methods are not reproducible. Reagent concentrations, equipment model in parentheses, Latin species names, informed consent, hazards, chronological order.
- **Results** — the culmination. Only results, as tables, figures or text; interpretation is deferred.
- **Discussion** — the hardest section, and where many papers are rejected despite good results. Restate purpose and hypothesis; report the findings whether or not the hypothesis held; generalise; explain what was and wasn't achieved; state limitations honestly; give practical recommendations; propose next steps; engage with other authors.

**Authorship:** only real contributors. Technicians and people who ran standard procedures go in Acknowledgements. For state scholarships, note that **sole authorship is scored higher** than co-authorship.

**Formatting convention used in Uzbek journals `[V]`:** title 14 pt bold ALL CAPS centred; section headings ALL CAPS 14 pt Times New Roman; body 14 pt; tables 10 pt labelled above as "1-jadval"; references 12 pt, line spacing 1.15; section names given trilingually (KIRISH / ВВЕДЕНИЕ / INTRODUCTION). Always check the target journal's own template — it overrides this.

**References:** APA (6th edition preferred) for Europe, the USA and much of Asia; GOST for Russian Federation journals. Build the list with Word's reference manager rather than by hand — manually typed lists are often not parsed by Scopus and RINC, so the citations don't count and the cited authors' h-index doesn't move. In-text numbers `[1]` must match list order. Uzbek- and Russian-language articles need a separate transliterated **REFERENCES** block in English for international indexing.

## Choosing a journal

**Scopus quartiles:** Q1 is the top 25% by CiteScore and the hardest; Q4 is the most accessible. The corpus's advice for a first publication is sound — **start at Q3–Q4 and climb**, rather than spending a year being rejected by Q1. Publishing at all is what unlocks the scholarship eligibility.

Expect: 20–30 Scopus-indexed references, peer review by two or three independent reviewers, roughly a month to a decision, and an **APC of $500–2,000** at many journals — though some are genuinely free.

**Predatory journals.** *"Aldovchi 'predator' jurnallardan ehtiyot bo'lish kerak — o'zini SCOPUS deb ko'rsatib, pul olib, aslida chop etmaydi."* Checks before paying anything: find the journal in the **official Scopus source list** (scopus.com/sources.uri) rather than trusting the journal's own claim; look at its published issues and whether real institutions appear in them; be suspicious of same-week acceptance, of email solicitations, and of any journal that accepts a paper without substantive review. For dissertation-relevant publication in Uzbekistan, the **OAK (VAK) list** of approved editions is the authority.

**Fake conferences and bought medals** belong in the same category — see the legitimacy section of `programs-and-requirements.md`. A medal bought for 600,000–900,000 UZS does not help a state scholarship or an international grant, and reviewers know the issuers.

## Review checklist for an article

- [ ] IMRAD order intact
- [ ] Title 3–15 words, no abbreviations
- [ ] Abstract 150–250 words covering purpose, method, results, conclusion
- [ ] 8–12 keywords
- [ ] Introduction states a specific gap, not just background
- [ ] Methods reproducible
- [ ] Results contain no interpretation
- [ ] Discussion states limitations
- [ ] References in the required style, built with a reference manager, in-text numbers matching
- [ ] Transliterated REFERENCES block if the article is in Uzbek or Russian
- [ ] Target journal verified against the official Scopus or OAK list
- [ ] Journal's own template followed, not a generic one
