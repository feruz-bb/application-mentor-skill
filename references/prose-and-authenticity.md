# Prose Quality and Authenticity

Load this alongside the document-specific reference whenever you are judging English writing. Two jobs here: make the prose stronger, and make sure it still sounds like the student.

## The authenticity problem

Selection committees now assume some applications are machine-written and screen for it. Some programs disqualify outright — YPIP states that a plagiarised personal essay voids the entire application. Beyond the rules, there is a practical detection: an essay that reads as far more sophisticated than the applicant's language score is a documented red flag, and interviews expose the gap immediately.

So the goal is never "make this sound better." It is **make this sound like the best version of this student.** A slightly awkward sentence carrying a real thought outperforms a polished one carrying nothing.

### Tells that a document was assembled rather than written

- Uniform, characterless fluency — every sentence the same length, every paragraph the same shape, no concrete nouns
- Abstractions doing the work of examples: "diverse opportunities," "invaluable experience," "cutting-edge research," "holistic development"
- Enthusiasm asserted rather than demonstrated: "I am deeply passionate about," "I am truly excited by"
- Nothing that could only be true of this person
- Scaffolding labels left visible: `HOOK:`, `Introduction:`, `Kirish:`, `Asosiy qism:`, `Xulosa:`
- Residue from another application — a different program's name, a stale date, a paragraph that contradicts the CV
- Person slippage between first and third person
- Claims the student could not expand on if asked one follow-up question

When you see these, say so directly and kindly. The fix is not more polish; it is going back for the specific detail that only the student has.

## Prose rules worth enforcing

### Orwell's six
1. Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

### Zinsser, operationalised
- **Cut.** "Most first drafts can be cut by 50 percent without losing any information or the author's voice." Bracket anything not doing work, then delete it.
- **Active verbs.** Passive constructions bury who did what. In an application document, *who did what* is the entire point.
- **Adverbs that duplicate the verb** — *blared loudly*, *grinned widely*, *clenched tightly*. Cut.
- **Adjectives that restate the noun** — *yellow daffodils*, *precipitous cliffs*. Cut.
- **Qualifiers.** Delete *very, quite, rather, a bit, sort of, kind of, pretty much, in a sense, somewhat*. "Don't be kind of bold. Be bold." Each one "whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust."
- **Long words down:** assistance→help · numerous→many · facilitate→ease · implement→do · sufficient→enough · attempt→try · utilize→use · in order to→to · due to the fact that→because · with the possible exception of→except · at this point in time→now.
- **Throat-clearing.** Delete "It should be noted that," "I might add that," "It is interesting to note that."
- **Reach the period sooner.** A 45-word sentence usually hides two good sentences.
- **No exclamation marks** to signal your own emphasis or joke. "Humor is best achieved by understatement."
- **The two questions,** after every paragraph: *What am I trying to say? Have I said it?*

### Revision in two passes
**Large scale first:** is the purpose clear, is anything missing, is the order logical, what can be cut wholesale. **Small scale second:** paragraph transitions, sentence-length variety, clichés, mixed metaphors, repetition. Doing them in the reverse order means polishing sentences you will delete.

## Dead phrases in application writing

Flag these on sight — they appear in nearly every weak draft:

> "No matter where my future may lead me" · "it changed my life" · "the best thing that ever happened to me" · "I have always been passionate about" · "from a young age" · "I am a hardworking and responsible person" · "I work well both independently and in a team" · "this opportunity would be invaluable" · "broaden my horizons" · "I will do my best" · "words cannot express"

And the overwritten register: *"I look forward with great relish," "I shall add my huge contribution," "wondrous joy," "the untamed fire in my heart."* Hyperbole diminishes the story it decorates.

## Common ESL patterns in this corpus

Worth checking specifically, because they recur in real drafts from Uzbek applicants:

- **Article errors** — missing "the"/"a" before institutions and fields ("I studied at university," "I am interested in international law" vs "in the field of international law")
- **Subject–verb agreement** — "his interpersonal skills is perfect," "the students has"
- **Tense drift** inside a paragraph, especially past↔present perfect
- **Preposition choice** — "participated at," "graduated the university," "in the last year" vs "last year"
- **Comma splices** and very long chained sentences joined by "and"
- **Direct translation** of Uzbek/Russian constructions — "negotiating with problems," "dialing my future goals," "make a good impression to others"
- **Capitalisation** of common nouns mid-sentence
- **Misspelled institution and program names** — the most damaging category, because it reads as carelessness about the very thing being applied to. Always check the program name, the university name, and "Committee."

Correct these. But when you rewrite, keep the sentence roughly as complex as the student's own writing, not more.

## Register and level

Match the rewrite to the student's demonstrated English. If they have IELTS 5.5 and write plainly, hand back plain, correct English. Introducing "multifaceted," "endeavour" and "paradigm" makes the document *less* credible, not more, and it will not survive an interview.

Guidance the corpus gives students directly, and which is worth repeating to them: write your own draft first, even with mistakes — it will be yours; work on your writing skill; then fix the grammar with a tool. Use AI for ideas and for error-checking, not as the author.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Under the word limit, and not far under
- [ ] No dead phrases from the list above
- [ ] Active voice dominant
- [ ] No qualifier stacking
- [ ] Longest sentence under ~35 words, or deliberately long for effect
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary
- [ ] Concrete nouns and named things throughout
- [ ] Every paragraph has one job
- [ ] Program, university and people's names spelled correctly
- [ ] Consistent person and tense
- [ ] Register matches the applicant's demonstrated level
- [ ] Nothing the student could not defend in an interview
