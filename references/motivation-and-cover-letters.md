# Motivation Letters and Cover Letters

## Which document is this?

- **Motivation letter / letter of intent** — for a *program*: a scholarship, a university place, an exchange, a fellowship. Argues why this program and why you.
- **Cover letter** — for a *position*: a job, an internship, a vacancy with a job ID. Argues why this role and why you, and rides on top of a resume.
- **Personal statement** — a different genre; see `personal-statement-and-essays.md`. A motivation letter argues; a personal statement narrates. Students often submit one where the other was asked for.

If the call says "motivation letter" and the student wrote something with no addressee, no program name and no closing — they have written a personal statement, and that is the first finding.

## Length

Read the call first; it almost always states a limit, and the limit is the limit.

Common ranges when the call is silent: motivation letters run **400–800 words**, roughly one page. Short forum and conference applications often specify **250 words**; some internships cap at **500**. Cover letters are shorter, **250–450 words**. Going over is a mechanical failure; coming in far under (a 200-word answer to a 500-word question) reads as indifference — the corpus's own guidance is that using 100 of 250 allowed words means the document is incomplete.

## Structure

**Motivation letter:**

1. **Opening** — why you are writing, naming the exact program, degree and institution. A quote can work as an opening *only if the next sentence converts it into your own claim*; otherwise it is decoration. Never open with "My name is."
2. **Who you are now** — current status, degree, key credentials, in two or three sentences. Not a CV replay.
3. **Why this field** — the origin of the interest, stated through a concrete thing you did rather than an adjective about how passionate you are.
4. **Why this program specifically** — the load-bearing paragraph. Named courses, named faculty, named research centres, named events. See "the fit test" below.
5. **What you bring** — evidence, quantified.
6. **Where this leads** — short-term and long-term plans, with a geography. "I plan to work in human rights in government structures in Uzbekistan" beats "I want to develop myself."
7. **What you give back** — what the program gets from funding you. Committees fund propagation, not just study.
8. **Close** — "Looking forward to…", "Sincerely," full name, and contact details.

**Cover letter:**

1. **Subject line carrying the job title and Job ID** if one exists — `Application for Intern Drive Acoustics (Job ID: 141267)`.
2. **Addressee and the role**, named exactly as advertised.
3. **Experience, knowledge, skills** relevant to *this* posting.
4. **Why this organisation** — the paragraph that decides whether the letter works.
5. **Close** — availability, thanks, signature.

## The fit test

**Cover the program name with your thumb. Could this letter be sent to a different university with only the name swapped?** If yes, the letter has failed, whatever else is good about it. This is the most valuable single observation you can give a student, and most drafts fail it.

What failure looks like:
> ❌ *"What really draws me into the program is the comprehensive curriculum and an opportunity to interact with erudite academics."*

What passing looks like:
> ✅ *"…courses such as Intercultural Communication, International Economics, and European Union Enlargement and External Relations"* — naming the actual modules.
> ✅ Naming a specific person: *"the lecturer and Vice-Rector for International Relations, Mattos Boris"*.
> ✅ Naming events the institution hosts: *"the GLOBSEC Bratislava Forum and the Slovak Security Forum"*.
> ✅ **The contrast move** — the strongest of all: naming what is unavailable where they are and available where they are going. *"At Soonchunhyang University I can take only my major classes because all other faculty courses are conducted in Korean."* This makes the application logically necessary rather than merely desirable.

Getting this right requires the student to spend twenty minutes on the program's own website. If they haven't, that is the assignment — not more adjectives.

## Named mistakes

1. **Generic phrases doing the work of arguments.** "Men jamoa bilan yaxshi ishlayman", "mening motivatsiyam kuchli", "I am a hardworking and responsible person." These distinguish nobody.
2. **The letter as a CV in prose.** If it restates the CV line by line, it has wasted the one document that could say something the CV cannot. Avoid repeating CV content; use it as evidence for a claim instead.
3. **A numbered achievement list dropped into a paragraph.** `1. … 2. … 3. …` inside a letter is CV content in the wrong container.
4. **Hedged salutation.** *"Dear Admission Office/Admission Committe/ Selection Committe/ To whom it may concern,"* — the applicant never chose. Pick one. And spell "Committee" correctly.
5. **Editing scaffolding left in the text** — `Introduction:`, `Goal:`, `Kirish:`, `Asosiy qism:`, `Xulosa:` visible in a submitted document.
6. **Copy-paste across applications** — the same three paragraphs in two letters, or the previous program's name surviving into the new letter. Fatal when caught.
7. **Stale dates** — "available to start from January 2022."
8. **Selfish framing.** *"…is a major chance to enhance my personal image and curriculum vitae"*; *"being a scholarship holder enhances my self-esteem."* Committees fund outcomes in the world, not self-esteem.
9. **Overwrought register.** "I look forward with great relish"; "I shall add my huge contribution."
10. **No editing pass.** Spelling and grammar errors in a one-page document read as indifference, and that inference is not unfair.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Within the stated word limit, and close to it rather than far under
- [ ] Program, degree and institution named exactly and correctly, every time
- [ ] Single, correct salutation
- [ ] Opens with something other than "My name is" or a restatement of the prompt
- [ ] Fails the thumb test? — i.e. is there at least one paragraph that could not be sent elsewhere
- [ ] Names specific courses, faculty, centres, or events
- [ ] Short-term and long-term plans both present, and concrete
- [ ] A give-back paragraph
- [ ] Every claim about the applicant has a fact behind it
- [ ] Does not duplicate the CV
- [ ] No scaffolding labels, no stale dates, no other program's name
- [ ] Consistent first person, consistent tense
- [ ] Signed, with contact details
- [ ] Saved as PDF with a sensible filename

## Worked transformation

**Hozir:**
> "I am very interested in your Master's program because it is one of the best in Europe and will help me to develop myself. I am a hardworking student and I always achieve my goals."

**Muammo:** Two sentences, no information. "One of the best in Europe" is true of hundreds of programs and shows no research. "Hardworking" and "always achieve my goals" are unfalsifiable, so the reader discounts both. Nothing here could not be written by someone who had never heard of the program.

**Taklif:**
> "I am applying to the MSc in [PROGRAM] because of [YOZING: aniq modul nomi] and Professor [YOZING: ism]'s work on [YOZING: mavzu], which is the closest thing I have found to the [YOZING: o'z loyihangiz] project I ran at [university]. My own department offers no course in this area, so I have been reading toward it on my own for two years."

Note what the rewrite does: it cannot be written without doing the research, and it converts "hardworking" into a demonstrated two-year behaviour.
