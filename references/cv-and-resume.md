# CV and Resume

## CV or resume?

They are not the same document and students routinely send the wrong one.

- **CV** (*curriculum vitae*, "hayot yo'li") — the full professional biography: education, work, publications, awards, research. Used for **academic and scientific contexts**: grants, research programs, master's and PhD admission, conferences. Length is not capped; 2–3 pages is normal, more if the publication list justifies it.
- **Resume** (*rezyume*) — a short, targeted summary of experience and skills for **a specific job**. Maximum 1–2 pages, and everything irrelevant to that role is cut.

For scholarships, grants and university admission, the student needs a **CV**. For an internship at a company, a **resume**.

## Format

**Europass** (europass.eu) is the recommended default for European applications and for most grant programs, and its structure is what the corpus's own winning CVs use. It is not universally required — a US graduate program will accept a clean plain CV, and some fields prefer one — but if the student has no reason to depart from it, Europass is the safe answer and its section names are a good checklist regardless of the template chosen.

Observed Europass section order:

```
ABOUT ME
EDUCATION AND TRAINING
WORK EXPERIENCE
LANGUAGE SKILLS
SKILLS
CONFERENCES AND SEMINARS
PROJECTS
HONOURS AND AWARDS
COMMUNICATION AND INTERPERSONAL SKILLS
VOLUNTEERING
NETWORKS AND MEMBERSHIPS
PUBLICATIONS
HOBBIES AND INTERESTS
```

**CONFERENCES AND SEMINARS as its own top-level section** is worth pointing out to students applying to grants and mobility programs — it is where a lot of their real evidence lives, and it disappears if buried under "other activities."

Entry format:
```
DD/MM/YYYY – DD/MM/YYYY   City, Country
ROLE OR TITLE — Organisation
   what you did, verb-initial
   what changed because you did it
Link: https://…
```

## The entry test

A CV entry is either a **duty** or a **result**. Duties are invisible to a reader; results are the whole point.

❌ *"Part-time manager. Assisting customers with inquiries. Processing transactions."*
❌ *"Participated in an international conference in Istanbul."*

✅ *"Topic: … Contribution: … Skills: … Outcome: awarded second place for presenting solutions with global participants."*
✅ *"Selected among 200 delegates from about 20 countries; won 3rd place in the «Best Project» category."*

Numbers that are usually available and usually missing: team size led, number of participants or beneficiaries, selection ratio ("selected from 1,300 applicants"), percentage change, audience or readership, hours contributed, funds raised, GPA.

## Review checklist

**Blocking**
- [ ] Length appropriate — 1–2 pages for a resume, 2–3+ for an academic CV. A five-page CV for a three-day forum is a signal of poor judgement.
- [ ] Contact details present and professional: full name, phone with country code, a serious email address, city/country, LinkedIn.
- [ ] No typos in the student's own name, university name, or program name.
- [ ] Dates consistent and in one format; no gaps left unexplained; no overlaps that can't be true.
- [ ] Exported as PDF, not .docx — formatting breaks on the reviewer's machine otherwise.
- [ ] Filename is `Firstname_Lastname_CV.pdf`, not `cv final FINAL(2).pdf`.

**Content**
- [ ] Every entry states an outcome, not only a duty.
- [ ] Achievements are quantified wherever a number exists.
- [ ] GPA included if it is good; the corpus's own samples are inconsistent on this and the strong one includes it.
- [ ] Language section gives CEFR levels and certificate scores with dates (IELTS 6.5, 2025), not "fluent".
- [ ] Skills section is not a bare list of Office products. Zoom and Microsoft Word are not skills; the field-specific tools are.
- [ ] Content is **relevant to this application**. A grant in public health does not need the convenience-store job. Cut ruthlessly — irrelevant entries dilute the strong ones.
- [ ] Evidence links point to institutional sources (university page, organiser's site, journal) rather than only to the student's own Telegram or Instagram posts.
- [ ] Publications listed with journal name, year, volume/issue, pages, and co-authors.

**Consistency and voice**
- [ ] Written consistently in one person. Text pasted from a Telegram post ("our student made a speech") produces third-person sentences inside a first-person CV — a common and very visible defect.
- [ ] Verb tense consistent: past for finished roles, present for current ones.
- [ ] No personal data that invites bias or is simply clutter: date of birth, gender, marital status, photo — required by Europass and normal in Uzbekistan and much of Europe, but **remove for US and UK applications**, where they are discouraged.
- [ ] No claims the student cannot evidence if asked.

## Common findings, in order of how often they appear

1. Duties instead of results throughout.
2. Skills section that lists Microsoft Office.
3. No quantification anywhere.
4. Same CV sent to every program — nothing reordered, nothing cut.
5. Person slippage from pasted text.
6. Personal contact clutter (Instagram, WhatsApp, date of birth) in the header.
7. Course names listed under Education instead of results, thesis topic, or honours.
8. A .docx attachment instead of PDF.

## What to tell a student with a thin CV

The honest answer is that the CV is a record, not a rhetorical device — you cannot write your way out of an empty one. What you can do is stop under-reporting. Most thin CVs belong to students who did more than they listed: university competitions, volunteering, online certificates, a group project, tutoring, a Telegram channel they run, a translation they did. Get the full inventory first, then decide what belongs. If it is genuinely thin, the useful advice is forward-looking — the corpus's own list of what builds a portfolio: GPA, language certificate, personal projects, internships, olympiads, national and international competitions, volunteering, social projects, conferences and summer schools, articles, online courses.
